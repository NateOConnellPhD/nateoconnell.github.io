Looking ahead

Power ratings & projections

These are estimates of team strength: what would be expected if two teams played. They use performance throughout the season to estimate how strong each team is. They are not my rankings for seeding. For those, results matter more than what I project would happen—that is what the merit rankings are for.

Power ratings

The rating is measured in points. Zero is the model’s reference level for an average FBS team. A rating of +28 means an estimated 28-point advantage over that reference team on a field where neither team has home advantage.

If one team is rated +28 and another +20, the difference is 8 points: the starting estimate favors the first team by eight. The matchup tool accounts for the venue and other game-specific adjustments to produce a game prediction. Select a team below to explore its estimates.

Weekly paths are retrospective estimates using every game available at this snapshot, not predictions made during those weeks. Game-implied strength is the blended game margin plus the opponent’s estimated strength, minus venue and quarterback advantages. How these forecasts work

The model uses teams’ performances throughout the season to estimate their strength, accounting for the opponents they played and where the games took place. It starts with expectations informed by previous seasons and team information, then updates as this season’s evidence accumulates. A team’s estimated strength can change during the season.

A game prediction combines both teams’ estimated strengths with the matchup conditions. It includes a range of possible outcomes because even well-matched estimates cannot make one game certain. The merit rankings answer a different question: how teams should be ranked based on the results they have earned.

Statistical details

A posterior is the model’s updated picture after seeing the games. Each draw is one plausible set of team strengths, home advantages, and game variability. Comparing teams within the same draw keeps their uncertainty connected.

This forecasting model starts with prior expectations based on last season’s strength, roster talent, preseason AP points, returning offensive production, recruiting, coaching changes, transfer-portal points, and conference-era indicators. The coefficients are fitted to early-season game margins from earlier seasons only. FCS teams get their own prior from last season’s Division I results. As games accumulate, the model learns how far teams have moved from those expectations. Team strength follows a weekly random walk: more weeks ahead means more uncertainty about where a team will be. This information is used for predictions, not the merit rankings.

The fit blends 75% final scoring margin with 25% efficiency margin, derived from postgame win expectancy. When efficiency information is missing, that game uses its actual margin. The residual variability is rescaled afterward to forecast real score margins. Home advantage includes both a national effect and a school offset, informed by the preceding three seasons; neutral games get neither.

Win probabilities average a Student-t predictive distribution over the posterior. This allows unusually large margins and includes game noise. “Stronger team” probability only compares underlying strength, so it need not equal the chance of winning one game.

Individual scores add a separate scoring model learned from final results in the preceding three seasons and games already played this season. It learns scoring tendencies for each school and season, allowing sparse teams to borrow information from the wider league. For each simulated margin, it draws the lower team score and adds the margin to get the winner’s score. Both scores stay nonnegative, and their difference keeps the power model’s simulated margin.

The scoring extension uses an approximate posterior (a Laplace approximation), while the power model keeps its existing posterior. The new score ranges have not yet been calibrated in a historical forecast evaluation. Scores are modeled continuously, so decimals describe average points, not literal football scoring events. Rounded scores can tie even when one team has a higher win probability. The score model has no separate injury, weather, or possession forecast; the expected starter and selected venue affect scores through the margin model.

The quarterback model learns passer value per dropback while separating offense, defense, experience, and season effects. A starter’s adjustment compares his value with his team’s usual passer mix, multiplied by its expected dropbacks. The power model learns how strongly that adjustment translates into points. Both teams’ QB adjustments enter the predicted margin and win probability. QB values are conditional estimates; the forecast draws include uncertainty in their learned points multiplier, not a second simulation of QB values.

The power-rating table summarizes strength with each team’s usual passer mix. Team details show the expected starter’s contribution; matchup predictions include it.

Play-by-play is checked daily. Matchup forecasts automatically use the expected starter: the last game’s first passer unless an explicit status update names another player. This is an assumption, not an injury report. Team details show the starter and contribution used. With no current-season dropbacks the supplied rule gives zero adjustment.

Fit details

Daily refreshes reuse saved data and passing fits. New training inputs trigger a fit; a starter-only change updates forecasts using the saved fit. If checks fail, the previous forecast stays available. New-season inputs are checked automatically from July.

How past predictions compared with results

These saved checks cover the earlier power model, before the quarterback and individual-score extensions. They do not measure the current model’s accuracy.

Range of past errors

Error means predicted home-team margin minus actual home-team margin, in points. Zero is an exact margin prediction; positive values overestimate the home team’s margin and negative values underestimate it. An average near zero means overestimates and underestimates balance. The spread shows how far individual predictions were off.

The selected range contains the middle 80% or 95% of the observed errors in these past games. It is not a confidence interval for the average or a predictive interval for a particular future game.

Technical accuracy measures

Mean absolute error (MAE) averages the magnitude of each margin error, ignoring its sign. It measures error size, not directional bias, and is not an interval. Its approximate confidence interval uses your selected coverage and describes uncertainty about that average, clustering by season and week. A group with only one week has no estimable cluster-based interval.
