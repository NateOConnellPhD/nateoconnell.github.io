Reading the results

How to read the rankings

Start with a season and week. The table asks what teams have earned from the games played up to that point. The numbered ranking is a useful summary, but the uncertainty tells you how much evidence separates those teams.

What is a posterior?

We don’t know exactly how strong each team is or exactly how impressive its results have been. The model considers many plausible combinations of team strengths and game-to-game variation, then uses the scores to work out which combinations are better supported. That updated picture of what we know—and what we’re still unsure about—is the posterior distribution.

A draw is one sampled scenario from that distribution. In one draw, a team might have the sixth-highest merit; in another, it might be twelfth. Looking across thousands of draws shows whether its position is fairly clear or could reasonably be much higher or lower. These are possibilities under the model, not thousands of additional games that actually happened.

Mean and median
The mean is the average across the draws. The median is the middle value when the draws are put in order. The ranking starts with mean merit; a difference between the mean and median can indicate an uneven distribution.
90% and 50% intervals
These contain the middle 90% or 50% of the draws. A raw-rank interval of 4–15 says the model still supports a fairly wide range of positions, even if the table puts the team seventh. Wider intervals mean more uncertainty. They describe what the model supports given the available results, not a guarantee.
SD
Standard deviation: a summary of how spread out the draws are around their mean. Larger values mean more uncertainty for the same quantity. It is not a percentage or a measure of how inconsistent the team is from game to game.

What each table column means

Rank
The final ordering after applying the stated rules for merit, head-to-head results, and common opponents. Lower is better. Changing a rule shows your alternative ordering and keeps the saved rank for that reference underneath.
AP / CFP comparison
Choose a poll to see its ranking beside mine. A difference of +3 means the team is three places higher here; −3 means three places lower. NR means it is outside the poll’s Top 25, so no numerical difference is calculated. The Top 25 view includes teams in either ranking. Check the selected poll edition: Margin of Merit uses games through the selected week, while polls have their own week labels and release schedules. The following poll week is suggested, but you can choose any saved edition, including postseason and final polls. Polls may include games that these regular-season rankings exclude. Missing editions stay unavailable rather than borrowing a different week. Poll data come from CollegeFootballData.
Weekly change
How many places the team moved since the immediately preceding week: Week 11 compares with Week 10. ↑ means a higher rank, ↓ means a lower rank, and 0 means unchanged. New means the team had no rank last week. N/A means the previous week is missing or cannot be compared; the note below the table explains why. When you adjust the rules, the same settings are applied to both weeks.
W–L
Overall wins and losses, including FCS opponents. FCS–FCS games (excluding FCS playoffs) help estimate opponent strength, but FCS teams do not enter the published rankings. These rankings include regular-season games, conference championships, and Army–Navy; bowls and playoff games are excluded.
Merit
The credit a team has earned across its eligible games. Each win adds between zero and one point; each loss subtracts up to one point. Opponent strength, margin, and venue determine how much. The table shows the mean and a 90% interval. Higher is better. This is accumulated résumé credit, not a predicted score or a per-game average.
Reference team
A common yardstick for grading every team’s results. We use the selected strength position among FBS teams in each posterior draw: 12th by default, with 2nd, 4th, and 24th available. The school occupying that position can change across draws. These boundaries let you explore two-, four-, twelve-, and 24-team fields; they do not reproduce era-specific playoff selection or automatic bids. Changing the reference recalculates merit, SOS, merit comparisons, and the resulting rankings from the same fitted model. The underlying team-strength estimates stay the same.
SOS: strength of schedule
How often the selected reference team would be expected to lose against this schedule, accounting for where the games were played. An SOS of 30% means about three expected losses across ten games for that reference team. Higher means a harder schedule; it is not the actual team’s predicted loss rate. A stronger reference will generally find the same schedule easier.
Raw rank
The ordering by mean merit before the additional ranking rules. Its 90% interval shows how the team’s merit rank varies across draws. The posterior explorer also shows the mean and median of those draw-by-draw ranks; those need not equal its position when teams are sorted by mean merit.
P(top 10)
The share of posterior draws in which the team is in the top ten by raw merit, with ties shared fairly. For example, 80% means top-ten merit in about eight out of ten draws. This is not its chance of making the playoff or winning its next game. The explorer also reports top-eight and top-25 probabilities.

Merit, SOS, and raw-rank uncertainty combine leave-one-game-out calculations using an approximation. Team strength and the model parameters use the full-data posterior. These are estimates under stated assumptions, not exact facts about the teams.

Reading the posterior plots

The horizontal axis is the quantity you selected. A narrow distribution means the draws are concentrated; a broad one means more uncertainty. Colors identify the teams. The solid vertical line is the mean and the dashed line is the median.

In Density / probability mass, bars show where the draws fall. For a continuous quantity, probability is represented by the area of a bar, not its height alone. For separate rank values, the height gives the share of draws at that value. In Cumulative probability, the curve shows the fraction of draws at or below a value: 0.80 means 80%. The continuous curve is approximated between the saved bin boundaries.

Overlapping distributions show why a single numbered position can hide uncertainty. The paired merit probability below the plot directly compares the two teams across their joint merit draws. A value of 70% means the first team has higher merit in roughly seven out of ten draws. Comparisons count strictly greater merit. It does not mean a 70% chance of beating the other team on the field.

Team strength (θ)
Underlying strength on the scoring-margin scale, measured in points. A ten-point difference between two teams corresponds to a ten-point expected margin on a neutral field under the model. This helps put results in context; it is different from the merit a team has earned.
Division and conference effects
Teams are grouped by division and conference. The model estimates each conference’s level and how much teams vary around it, using this season’s results. Every conference receives the same prior; no conference is assigned a bonus. Independents each have their own cluster. The FBS division baseline is set to zero, and the FCS baseline is estimated relative to it. The explorer shows the uncertainty in these estimates.
National home advantage
The shared home effect, in points. The preceding three completed seasons help estimate it, and current-season results update that estimate. Historical games inform home effects only; this season’s team strengths start fresh.
School home offset and advantage
An FBS host’s home advantage is the national effect plus its own estimated offset. School offsets are pulled toward zero when there is little evidence for a difference. FCS hosts use the national effect, and neutral sites receive no home advantage. The historical estimates use a Gaussian approximation, with uncertainty carried into the current model.
Residual scale (σ)
How much scoring margins tend to vary around what team strengths and venue suggest. Larger values allow more unpredictable results. It is a scale parameter, not literally the standard deviation. The model uses an eight-point minimum throughout the season.
Degrees of freedom (ν)
How readily the model allows unusually extreme margins. Smaller values give more room for surprising blowouts rather than forcing every score to fit a narrow pattern.

What happens when I change the rules?

The controls in the Rules tab change how the final ordering is constructed. They keep the merit estimates, SOS, and uncertainty for the selected reference unchanged. The Reference team selector above the tabs changes those calculations too. A threshold of 90% is a decision rule, not a 90% bonus to a team’s score.

Merit protection
A probability at or above this threshold creates a preference to keep the higher-merit team ahead. It can yield to qualifying actual results when the requirements conflict.
Head-to-head
A decisive direct result qualifies when the loser’s probability of greater merit is below the chosen threshold OR the teams are within the chosen number of places in the current ranking. The default probability threshold is 95%. With the default two-place window, an adjacent team’s win qualifies even against 99% opposing merit confidence.
Common opponents
For teams that never played each other, one must have at least as good a win fraction against every shared FBS opponent and a better one against at least one. The result qualifies through the probability threshold OR current-rank proximity. Any direct meeting rules out this fallback, including a split series.
Current-ranking proximity
Distance in the current order. We start with mean merit, apply the qualifying results, and check the new order again. If a comparison comes within two places, it becomes eligible and stays eligible. This repeats until no new comparisons qualify. It does not limit how far a team can move.
Equal records
This switch limits head-to-head and common-opponent comparisons to equal overall records, including FCS games. An undefeated record does not guarantee a higher rank: a team with losses can rank above an undefeated team based on earned merit. No ordering rule gives a conference special treatment.

Rules can conflict. The final order satisfies as many qualifying direct results as possible, then common-opponent results, then merit preferences. Among equally good solutions, it makes the fewest pairwise changes to the raw ranking. “Explain rank” shows the evidence for the selected pair and any overridden requirements. It is possible to change a threshold without moving any teams. The reset button restores the published settings. Every season and every week uses these same rules.

Reading the weekly chart

Each point uses only results through that week’s cutoff. For ranks, higher on the chart means a better position; for merit, it means more earned credit. The vertical ranges show 90% uncertainty intervals when you select a posterior summary. Missing fits leave gaps. Weeks awaiting a refit remain unavailable until they pass the same checks as the current rankings.

Historical weeks are reconstructed from saved game data. An early-season reconstruction lets you explore how little was known at the time; a numbered position on its own is not evidence that the teams were clearly distinguishable.

What are the diagnostic checks?

These are checks on the computation. R̂ asks whether independent sampling runs agree; values near one are reassuring. Effective sample size (ESS) estimates how much independent information the draws provide, because neighboring draws can be related. “Bulk” refers to the main part of the distribution and “tail” to its extremes. Divergences and tree-depth limit hits flag sampling difficulties that need attention.

Leave-one-game-out means evaluating a game using estimates made without that game, so it does not help grade itself. PSIS is a shortcut for that calculation. Flagged games need a separate omitted-game fit when the shortcut is unreliable. The transport mean error checks how closely the combined draws preserve the per-game average credits. Passing numerical checks does not prove the model’s assumptions are correct.

Behind the rankings

How it works

The best team isn’t necessarily the most deserving team. I built Margin of Merit to evaluate what teams have earned on the field: who they played, whether they won, and by how much. Every season starts fresh.

In College Football Has a Rankings Problem. So I Built My Own., I explain why I wanted a different approach, how the Bayesian model learns from the network of games, and how those results become merit, schedule strength, and an ordering with uncertainty.
