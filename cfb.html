Behind the rankings

How it works

I wanted a ranking that starts with what happened on the field. A win over a strong opponent should mean something different from a win over a weak one, and the margin and location should matter too. Margin of Merit adds up what a team earns from each game, using a common reference opponent standard across the season.

Each game contributes to a team’s résumé

The model estimates team strength and home advantage from scoring margins. For each game, it asks how a reference team—the 25th-strongest team in that posterior draw—would be expected to perform against that opponent, in that location. The actual margin is converted into a percentile of that distribution. Wins contribute between zero and one point; losses contribute between minus one and zero. A team’s season merit is the sum of those game credits.

The game being scored is left out when estimating its opponents and reference standard, so it does not help grade itself. The rankings use completed regular-season FBS-versus-FBS games, including conference championships and Army–Navy. FCS games and bowls/CFP are excluded.

Strength of schedule, with uncertainty

The same model lets me ask how difficult each team’s schedule has been. For each game, I estimate the probability that the reference team—the 25th-strongest team in that draw—would lose against that opponent, in that location. I average those probabilities across the team’s eligible games to get strength of schedule (SOS). Everyone is evaluated using the same reference rule, and higher values mean a harder schedule.

An SOS of 30% means the reference team would be expected to lose 30% of the games on that schedule: three losses over ten games, for example. That is different from predicting how the actual team would perform. The rankings table shows the posterior mean and 90% interval; select “Strength of schedule (SOS)” in the posterior explorer for its distribution, median, standard deviation, and team comparisons. As with merit, the uncertainty uses the saved marginal-quantile leave-one-out approximation.

Let the uncertainty tell us when results should decide

V4 starts with posterior mean merit. I don’t define “close” by how many places separate two teams. I use the probability that one has earned more merit than the other. Two teams twelve places apart can qualify for a head-to-head adjustment; two neighboring teams can have enough evidence separating them to keep their order protected.

At the official defaults, a merit comparison above 90% probability is protected. For teams with the same FBS record, a decisive head-to-head winner must go ahead unless the model gives the loser more than a 90% chance of higher merit, or another required ordering prevents it. Common opponents are considered only when the teams never played each other. A team that did at least as well against every shared opponent and better against one must go ahead unless opposing merit confidence exceeds 70%, or that ordering conflicts with an earlier requirement. A split direct series never sends us looking for a common-opponent tiebreak.

There’s one separate policy choice: an undefeated ACC, Big Ten, Big 12, or SEC team with at least eight FBS games stays ahead of teams with two or more FBS losses. That can override merit protection for those comparisons. You can change the three thresholds, compare across different records, or turn off that undefeated-team rule above.

These rules create required pairwise orderings, not swaps of numbered positions. If a rule would create a contradiction, its explanation shows the full chain blocking it. Among teams whose requirements are satisfied, the team with the highest raw merit goes next. Changing the rules may move teams—or leave the order exactly as it was.

The uncertainty is part of the result

The intervals show how much the underlying merit and rank vary across posterior draws. “P(top 10)” is the probability that a team’s raw merit falls in the top ten. It is not a playoff probability, and it does not apply the editorial ordering rules to each draw. The joint uncertainty uses a marginal-quantile approximation, so these probabilities should not be read as exact.

Model and update details

Scoring margins follow a Student-t model with team-strength differences and home advantage. Team strengths have Normal(0, 14) priors; home advantage has a Normal(2, 2.5) prior. The residual scale is 8 + exp(η), with η distributed Normal(log 6, 0.75). Degrees of freedom are 2 + exp(ζ), with ζ distributed Normal(log 5, 0.9). Normal scales here are standard deviations. The eight-point floor is on the Student-t scale, not its standard deviation, and stays the same all season.

Fits use four sequential NUTS chains and checked leave-one-game-out scoring. Unreliable importance-sampling approximations require individual omitted-game refits. V4 uses no preference weights or rank-distance penalties. Direct-result requirements precede common-opponent requirements; within either type, lower opposing-merit probability comes first, with ties resolved by stable team names. Contradictory hard requirements are reported as an error. Stable topological completion respects every accepted requirement but does not promise the smallest possible number of rank changes.

Changing a control reuses saved pairwise probabilities in your browser. It does not rerun the Bayesian model. Earlier archived updates keep the policy used when they were published; controls are available on v4 snapshots.

Game data come from CollegeFootballData. Updates are scheduled for Sunday mornings, with additional refreshes when needed. A week may still have unplayed games: use the displayed observation time and game count to see what an update includes. If an update fails its checks, the last successful rankings stay available. Historical reconstructions use observed final scores and kickoff cutoffs, rather than an exact record of when each game finished.
